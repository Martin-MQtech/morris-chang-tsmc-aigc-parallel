<p class="en-para">In 2011, our finance organization and the 'market forecasting' unit established in the 1990s had both become quite mature. The former had the backing of Goldman Sachs; the latter not only forecast the semiconductor market but also the major electronic-product markets that used semiconductors, such as smartphones. Within a few weeks of discussing matters with both advisory units and relevant senior managers, I decided to pursue options (1) and (4).</p>
<p class="en-para">Regarding option (1), we decided to commit to only about half of the demand estimate Apple had given us. Morris Wei, who had been leading the business development organization for just over a year, was given the 'honor' of being the first to tell Apple the news. His response at Apple was: 'Are you crazy? You are only accepting half of our demand estimate?'</p>
<p class="en-para">We were not crazy. This was the decision we reached after conducting prudent financial planning. In June 2011, I personally went to California to meet with Jeff and repeated the message Morris had already delivered to his team. Jeff merely remarked, in a casual tone, 'You could always suspend dividends for a while. I think your shareholders would understand.' I responded in kind: 'A significant portion of our shareholders—roughly thirty percent in fact—place considerable importance on dividends.' He then said no more.</p>
<p class="en-para">Apple committed only to half its estimated demand in wafers. Even with the quantity halved, TSMC still needed to issue seven-point-one billion dollars in corporate bonds. After discussions between our finance department and Goldman Sachs, we decided to issue a total of one hundred sixty-two billion New Taiwan dollars in corporate bonds across Taiwan in 2011, 2012, and 2013, and additionally to issue fifteen billion dollars in corporate bonds in New York in 2013.</p>
