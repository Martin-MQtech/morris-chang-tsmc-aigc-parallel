> **"Straight-Line" Investment: The Strategy That Made the Difference**
>
> The capital expenditure table tells a powerful story: from 1992 to 2000, TSMC invested aggressively yet still could not keep up with demand—capacity was perpetually insufficient. During the 2001-2003 downturn, we held our ground rather than retrenching. From 2004 to 2009, we invested steadily at approximately $2.5 billion annually. In 2010-2012, recognizing our emerging technology leadership and the revenue opportunities it would bring, we ramped capital investment dramatically to over $8 billion. And from 2013 through my retirement in 2018, we sustained an annual capex run rate of roughly $10 billion.
>
> The "straight-line" investment approach was a triumph, dovetailing beautifully with TSMC's integrated competitive advantage of "technology leadership, manufacturing excellence, and client trust." It delivered both steady growth and rising market share.
>
> **The 2001-2003 Downturn and the Three Principles of Recession Management**
>
> When the storm hit, I implemented a set of recession-management principles that I had first articulated years earlier at TI but never had the authority to execute fully:
>
> First, maintain partnerships with customers and suppliers. Demand collapsed suddenly. Clients who had been clamoring for supply suddenly halved their orders. The long-term trajectory of the semiconductor industry remained positive, I argued, but the present downturn was transitory. We needed to prepare and be ready to surge when the tide turned.
>
> Second, no layoffs. The Japanese tradition of lifetime employment, I believed, fostered deep employee loyalty. Moreover, layoffs followed within a year by new hiring made no economic sense—severance typically ran to half a year's salary, and training new hires cost the same. In fact, headcount at the end of 2001 was only marginally below year-end 2000 levels, as we let natural attrition take its course.
>
> Third, increase, do not cut, R&D investment. TSMC's R&D budget climbed from NT$5.1 billion in 2000 to NT$10.6 billion in 2001 and NT$11.7 billion in 2002. When competitors retrench, that is precisely when talent becomes most available.
