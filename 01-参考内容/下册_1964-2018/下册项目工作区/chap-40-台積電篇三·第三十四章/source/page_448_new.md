<p class="en-para">I began planning the succession of TSMC's chairman and CEO positions as early as 2005. The original plan was to first release the CEO role to Morris Cai, while I retained the chairmanship. If Morris succeeded smoothly as CEO, I could retire completely after a few years. But business and personnel matters at TSMC did not go smoothly in the following years, so in 2009 I resumed the CEO role. I never abandoned the possibility that Morris would eventually succeed me, so I appointed him general manager of TSMC's new businesses. Unfortunately, TSMC's new businesses did not fare well, and Morris resigned in 2014 to become chairman of Chunghwa Telecom. He later moved to MediaTek in 2017 as co-CEO, where he performed excellently and is now vice chairman and CEO.</p>
<p class="en-para">After resuming the CEO role for several years, by 2012 I felt that TSMC had been brought back onto a solid path (see Chapters 32 and 33). The question of succession arose again in my mind. Morris Cai was still a possible candidate; his pay and rank remained those of TSMC's CEO. But years had passed, and a few more potential successors should be promoted.</p>
<p class="en-para">In 2012, the most obvious leadership talents within the company were, in alphabetical order by surname: (1) Sandi Jin—then in charge of R&amp;D; (2) Tim Liu—then in charge of manufacturing; (3) Morris Wei—then in charge of the business development organization.</p>
<p class="en-para">In March 2012, I promoted Sandi, Tim, and Morris to co-chief operating officers. Perhaps more importantly, I told them: 'The three of you will rotate responsibility for the company's three major domains'—namely R&amp;D, manufacturing, and business development, as described above. From the start, I transferred Sandi away from R&amp;D and assigned him to manufacturing.</p>
