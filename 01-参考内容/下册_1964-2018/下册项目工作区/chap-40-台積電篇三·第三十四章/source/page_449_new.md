<p class="en-para">If the system of three rotating leaders across the three major domains had been fully implemented in 2012, perhaps TSMC's succession six years later—when I retired in 2018—would have been different. Of course, this is only a 'perhaps.' The course of events is infinite and cannot be predicted by anyone.</p>
<p class="en-para">What I failed to implement was the 'rotation' system I had initially intended. In 2012, TSMC's R&amp;D department was working hard to develop the first process node required by Apple—twenty nanometer—a quite challenging node. After transferring Sandi away from R&amp;D, problems kept arising in the twenty-nanometer development (which is inherent to new technology development). I seemed never fully at ease. I decided, never mind, to send Sandi back to R&amp;D. Sandi returned to his original post, while Tim and Morris swapped: Tim took over marketing and business development, and Morris took over manufacturing.</p>
<p class="en-para">Of course I am quite familiar with the talents within the company. But there was one person outside the company, and under every condition—in character, vision, and expertise and knowledge in semiconductors—worth considering as a TSMC successor. I should also talk to him.</p>
<p class="en-para">That person was Jensen Huang. By 2013 I had known him for sixteen or seventeen years. So I asked him whether he had any interest in the TSMC CEO position, and if so, we could discuss further.</p>
<p class="en-para">I spent about ten minutes giving Jensen a concise explanation of my deep expectations for TSMC, and also explained the compensation for TSMC's CEO—although there were no TSMC shares, the fixed salary plus the annual bonus tied to company net profit would certainly exceed his then-current income.</p>
<p class="en-para">Jensen seemed to listen patiently, but he did not ask any questions. When I finished, he did not directly refuse me either. He simply answered: 'I already have a job.'</p>
