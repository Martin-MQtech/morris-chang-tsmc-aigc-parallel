**Releasing and Reclaiming the CEO Position**

As the internet bubble-induced semiconductor downturn ended in Q3 2003, and 2004 brought record revenues and profits, the outlook appeared clear. Now seventy-four, I confronted the question of succession. My first step: relinquishing the CEO title while retaining the chairmanship.

The term "CEO" is an American invention dating to the late eighteenth century, attributed to Alexander Hamilton. When establishing the First Bank of the United States, Hamilton borrowed the constitutional title of Chief Executive for the bank's head, appending "Officer" to distinguish the role from the presidency. The common Chinese translation "执行长" is seriously misleading—a CEO's mandate extends far beyond "execution." He must engage with the external world (geopolitics, customers, competitors), mobilize the company's resources, and serve as its primary face to the outside world.

In May 2005, I proposed to the board that Tsai Li-hsing (Morris Tsai) become CEO. Four years later, in 2009, following the global financial crisis and a controversial layoff handled unilaterally by Tsai without board approval, I reclaimed the CEO role to restore trust, rebuild morale, and reestablish the principle that fundamental decisions must go through the board.

The episode also deepened my conviction: without proper oversight, even well-intentioned management decisions can inflict lasting damage. The board's supervisory function must never be merely a formality.
