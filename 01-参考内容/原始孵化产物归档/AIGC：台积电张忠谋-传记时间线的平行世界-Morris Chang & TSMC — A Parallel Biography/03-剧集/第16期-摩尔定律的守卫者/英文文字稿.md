## Opening

[SFX: the hum of robotic arms moving in a cleanroom, fine and unceasing, like a march that never ends]

The year 2017. Hsinchu Science Park, Taipei.

[Main narrator]

Inside TSMC's headquarters, a freshly released set of numbers made everyone hold their breath.

That year, TSMC's revenue approached one trillion New Taiwan dollars, and its operating profit exceeded three hundred and eighty billion. In nine years, it had taken its revenue from three hundred billion all the way up to nearly one trillion — more than triple.

[Main narrator, slower]

And more astonishing than the numbers was something else: it already held the key to the next-generation process — seven nanometers.

[Main narrator]

Seven nanometers. That year, it was the most dangerous and most valuable technical gate in the world. Not one other chipmaker on earth could pull it off at that moment.

To understand the weight of seven nanometers, you first have to know a law.

[Main narrator, slower]

In 1965, an Intel engineer named Gordon Moore wrote down an observation in a magazine: the number of transistors on a chip doubles roughly every two years.

[Main narrator]

That observation later got a name — Moore's Law.

It is not a law of nature. It is a promise — a promise that the entire semiconductor industry spent fifty years keeping together.

To keep that promise, TSMC had been chasing for a full decade.

[Main narrator, slower]

And what drove it onward was exactly this law, slowly growing old.

## Act One · A Retirement Plaque

To tell this chase properly, you have to begin with one person.

[Main narrator]

On October 31, 2013, TSMC's R&D general Chiang Shang-yi formally retired.

This man joined TSMC in 1997, and from day one he was TSMC's head of R&D — the first R&D chief to whom Morris Chang entrusted the mission of "technology leadership."

[Main narrator, slower]

In 2006, the year Rick Tsai took over as CEO, Chiang Shang-yi chose to retire and leave. In 2009, when Morris Chang returned as CEO, he personally brought him back over a bowl of beef noodles.

[Main narrator]

That bowl of beef noodles was about loyalty and about duty — because a forty-nanometer yield crisis was gripping TSMC's throat.

[Main narrator, slower]

The crisis was fierce. In the summer of 2009, TSMC's forty nanometers had just entered mass production, but the yield was frighteningly low — at one point only twenty or thirty percent. Customers were waiting with empty hands, and orders piled into mountains.

[Main narrator]

It was Chiang Shang-yi who came back, reorganized a team, and threw himself onto the production line day and night until the yield was pulled up, bit by bit.

The twenty-eight-nanometer battle was the true watershed that left Intel and Samsung behind — Qualcomm, Nvidia, and Apple all fought for capacity, and only TSMC had any to give.

[Main narrator, slower]

But just as TSMC was about to push still deeper, he reached the company's mandatory retirement age: sixty-seven.

[Main narrator]

The retirement dinner was warm. Morris Chang personally handed him a plaque bearing four characters — "Merit to TSMC."

[Main narrator, slower]

In Morris Chang's memory, those four characters were the only plaque he ever gave with such praise.

[Main narrator]

The only one. What does that mean?

It means that in his heart, half of TSMC's technical empire was carved out, inch by inch, by this man and his brothers. Chiang Shang-yi was the kind of R&D talent who was brilliant, with a strong sense of technical direction and real leadership — only, he was also extremely sensitive. And that was exactly what Morris Chang respected most: a man who understood technology, and held its dignity above all else.

But a man may retire; the war goes on. Should TSMC keep chasing Moore's Law?

[Main narrator, slower]

Morris Chang did not hesitate.

Because the answer had been written on another piece of paper three years earlier.

## Act Two · Eight Percent for R&D

That piece of paper held a number.

[Main narrator]

As early as 2010, Morris Chang laid down an iron rule: TSMC would put eight percent of its revenue into R&D every year.

That number was something he had wanted — and failed to get — back in his Texas Instruments days.

[Main narrator, slower]

He had proposed raising R&D spending from four point seven percent to five point five percent. His superiors rejected it, again and again, whittling it down.

[Main narrator]

It was one of the most frustrating things of his younger years. Nothing torments an engineer-turned-manager more than watching money fail to flow into the place it most needs to go.

Now, at TSMC, he had the final say. He wrote down eight percent, and no one dared cut it.

[Main narrator, slower]

Eight percent. What does that even mean?

[Main narrator]

By 2023, TSMC's annual R&D spending reached five point six billion US dollars — more than MIT's entire annual budget.

Not one cent of that money went into anything "visible." It went into lithography machines that cost an obscene fortune, and into process nodes that got harder to nudge forward by a single inch, generation after generation.

[Main narrator, slower]

From forty nanometers, to twenty-eight, twenty, sixteen, twelve, ten — in nine years TSMC pushed them out, one generation after another.

[Main narrator]

How hard was the pushing? Morris Chang admitted it himself: the essence of developing new technology is that problems keep appearing, and you have to press them down, over and over.

And if you can't press them down? Then you bring back the man who knows technology best, and let him press again.

What did that eight percent actually buy?

[Main narrator, slower]

In 2012, TSMC's twenty-eight nanometers had just entered mass production, and chip design companies around the world swarmed in at almost the same instant.

[Main narrator]

Qualcomm's orders went from forty thousand wafers a month, to a hundred and twenty thousand, then to three hundred thousand. Qualcomm's own chairman and president flew to Hsinchu in person to tell Morris Chang: we just want more chips, and we are willing to pay a higher price.

[Main narrator, slower]

That is the return on eight percent for R&D — when Moore's Law has pushed everyone to the edge of a cliff, only the one holding the latest process has the right to stand on that cliff and wait for customers to line up.

[Main narrator]

At the end of Moore's Law stands the one still willing to pay, and still willing to run.

## Act Three · A Costlier and Costlier Arms Race

But not everyone was willing to keep paying.

[Main narrator]

After 2010, the semiconductor industry entered a brutal arms race. Every step down the process ladder sent costs up another notch.

The lithography machine was the most expensive ticket in this race.

[Main narrator, slower]

ASML of the Netherlands sells one of its most advanced EUV — extreme ultraviolet — machines for over a hundred million euros, and it weighs like a railway car. Moving one takes several containers and more than a dozen Boeing cargo flights, just to get it from Europe into a Hsinchu cleanroom.

[Main narrator]

And yet you cannot do without it. Because by the seven-nanometer generation, the old way of lithography had hit a physical wall.

So the rivals who once stood shoulder to shoulder began to drop out, one by one.

[Main narrator, slower]

Listen first to Intel's voice.

[Main narrator]

This company was once the king of semiconductors. But what it believed in was a single chain — design, manufacture, and test all under one roof. When the world outsourced its manufacturing, it refused. When its proudest node, ten nanometers, was delayed again and again, and in April 2018 it announced yet another postponement of mass production, it suddenly found: it had neither enough customers to share the cost of that astronomically expensive factory, nor enough orders to amortize a generation of process development. And the lithography route it insisted on left it a full generation late to EUV.

[Main narrator, slower]

One step slow, every step slow.

[Main narrator]

Now Samsung's voice.

Samsung had ambition, and it had money. It rushed to be the first to trial-produce seven nanometers with EUV, hoping one bold gamble would pull TSMC down. But extreme ultraviolet lithography is brutally hard — thirteen-point-five-nanometer light, passing through mirror after mirror; the slightest deviation, and the yield collapses. Samsung won the title of "first to try," yet was stopped at the gate of mass production by yield. Sometimes the one who sprints first is not the one who reaches the finish line.

[Main narrator, slower]

Last, GlobalFoundries' voice.

[Main narrator]

It had neither Intel's accumulation nor Samsung's war chest. It had to fight three giants for the same customers, while answering to the patience of the money behind it. Seven-nanometer R&D was a bottomless pit — and its funds could not sustain an endless money-burning game.

[Main narrator, slower]

Three rivals, three ways of dropping out.

[Main narrator]

And TSMC chose to keep doubling down.

## Act Four · A Bet of Over Ten Billion

Doubling down means putting real money on the table.

[Main narrator]

A wafer fab is the most expensive factory on earth. A single state-of-the-art twelve-inch fab costs on the order of ten billion US dollars.

TSMC's capital expenditure climbed steadily from five point nine billion dollars in 2010. By 2016, it crossed ten billion for the first time; in 2018, it was raised to somewhere between ten point five and eleven billion.

[Main narrator, slower]

Over ten billion dollars a year, for one goal alone: to make seven nanometers before anyone else.

[Main narrator]

Seven nanometers was the most dangerous — and most valuable — gate Moore's Law had reached that year. Only three and a half companies on earth could still walk that road: TSMC, Samsung, Intel, and GlobalFoundries, struggling to keep up.

Morris Chang's arithmetic was clear.

[Main narrator, slower]

In technology, TSMC conceded nothing to anyone; in manufacturing cost, he was confident of beating Intel; in customer service, it was the very craft TSMC had built itself on.

[Main narrator]

So he made the call: keep chasing. Chase until only TSMC was left running.

And along the way, there were two things he quietly buried as foreshadowing.

[Main narrator, slower]

One was called InFO, the other CoWoS. Two kinds of chip packaging.

[Main narrator]

InFO was critical to smartphones; and CoWoS, in Morris Chang's own words — was critical to artificial intelligence.

Note those words. In 2017, the vast majority of people could not yet see how big AI would grow. But this eighty-six-year-old man had already paved the road for it.

## Act Five · Seven Nanometers, Alone at the Front

In 2017, the answer came out.

[Main narrator]

That year, TSMC's revenue approached one trillion New Taiwan dollars, and its operating profit exceeded three hundred and eighty billion. And its seven nanometers was already ready in 2017, prepared for mass production the following year.

This was something no one else on earth had yet done.

[Main narrator, slower]

In May 2018, TSMC began mass-producing a chip called the A12 for Apple — the world's first smartphone processor made on seven nanometers.

[Main narrator]

Orders poured in like snow; capacity could not meet demand.

[Main narrator, slower]

That A12 was the "heart" Apple had prepared for the 2018 iPhones. It was not just a chip — it was the farthest boundary mobile computing could reach that year.

[Main narrator]

Apple placed its most expensive order on TSMC's seven nanometers — because apart from TSMC, there was no second company that could make seven nanometers on time, make it stable, and make it in volume.

That year, TSMC's full-year revenue broke through one trillion thirty-one billion New Taiwan dollars, another record high; and its global foundry market share climbed to fifty-six percent. Its seven-nanometer capacity, by its own annual report, had doubled compared with 2017 — and was still climbing.

[Main narrator, slower]

Then, a heavy hammer fell.

[Main narrator]

On August 27, 2018, the struggling GlobalFoundries suddenly announced: it would indefinitely halt development of seven nanometers.

The moment the news broke, the whole industry understood what it meant. One analyst did the math on the spot: by the end of 2018, TSMC's share of seven nanometers would be — ninety-five percent.

[Main narrator, slower]

Ninety-five percent. Not a lead. It was — the only one left.

[Main narrator]

Morris Chang wrote that moment with extreme lightness, and extreme weight, in his memoir. He said TSMC had rolled out seven generations of process, leaving rivals far behind, and "rivals began to announce that they would stop chasing."

[Main narrator, slower]

"Stop chasing" — those three words were the cruelest, and loudest, verdict at the end of Moore's Law.

[Main narrator, time capsule]

His year: 2017 — seven nanometers leads the world alone, and rivals fall far behind.

The world's year: Moore's Law grows old, and chip companies everywhere ask the same question — should we keep chasing?

One timeline — the world was asking whether to chase; he was already at the end of the chase.

## Coda · A Parallel View

Morris Chang chased for sixty years, and chased out an answer.

[Main narrator]

From entering Sylvania at twenty-four, to the year he was eighty-seven, still listening at TSMC board meetings to reports on seven-nanometer progress.

Sixty years, and he had seen too many people stop halfway.

[Main narrator, slower]

Some charged too hard when the market was good, and died in the next winter. Some hesitated at a bend in the technology, and never caught up again. Others simply announced: we stop chasing.

[Main narrator]

He, meanwhile, simply kept his head down and pushed Moore's Law forward, generation after generation. Until at last the whole world saw one thing clearly: whether this law can continue does not depend on physics — it depends on whether there is still anyone willing to run for it.

Someone asked him: will Moore's Law have an end?

[Main narrator, slower]

He had already answered with his own actions long ago: even if there is an end, the one standing at that end is the one still willing to run.

[Main narrator]

And the seed he had planted for artificial intelligence — that CoWoS — was about to bloom a few years later.

By then, a company that made no chips and only drew blueprints would rise quietly on TSMC's process, and become the new king of the AI age. Its name was Nvidia.

[Main narrator, slower]

And the start of it all is hidden in this very episode — when the whole industry thought the story of the chip was nearing its end, Morris Chang and his TSMC, with an eight percent and a bet of over ten billion, stretched that law forward another ten years.

[Main narrator]

But that is a story for later.

First, in the next episode, we must send this old man down from the battlefield he has guarded for sixty years — and the last thing he has to do is hand the baton on.

[Main narrator, slower]

What he handed over after sixty years on that battlefield was not just TSMC, but an answer: in this industry, the one who runs the longest is not the most gifted, but the one who never steps off the field midway.

[Main narrator]

That is the Guardian of Moore's Law.

## Knowledge Extension · Historical Background Cards

1. **Moore's Law**: In 1965, Gordon Moore observed that transistor counts on a chip double roughly every two years — a fifty-year shared promise of the industry, not a law of physics.
2. **The process-node arms race**: By the late 2010s, 10nm/7nm became a life-or-death line for fabs; Intel's 10nm was delayed again and again, while Samsung bet on EUV but was held back by yield.
3. **Seven nanometers, alone at the front**: In May 2018 TSMC mass-produced the Apple A12 (the world's first 7nm smartphone chip); on August 27, 2018, GlobalFoundries announced it would halt 7nm, leaving TSMC with roughly 95% of 7nm share.
4. **Eight percent R&D and capex over ten billion**: TSMC set annual R&D at 8% of revenue (reaching $5.6B in 2023, more than MIT's entire budget); capex first crossed $10B in 2016 and reached $10.5–11B in 2018.
5. **InFO and CoWoS**: Two packaging technologies — the first vital to smartphones, the second critical to artificial intelligence — foreshadowing the coming AI wave.
