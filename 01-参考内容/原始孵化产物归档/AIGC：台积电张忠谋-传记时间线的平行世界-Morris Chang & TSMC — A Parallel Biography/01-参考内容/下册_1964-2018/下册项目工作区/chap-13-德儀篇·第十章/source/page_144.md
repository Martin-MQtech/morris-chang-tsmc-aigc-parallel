实际上，家庭电脑只是夏伯特「到处都有计算」（Ubiquitous Computation）理念的一部分。夏伯特的理念中，家庭电脑用来算家庭帐、报个人所得税、教育儿童、储藏食谱等等；「商业电脑」放在办公室，用来做专业工作。

夏伯特的理念很有远见，但比现实世界至少早了十几年！在现实世界里，IBM在一九八一年推出「个人电脑」，把「个人电脑」正统化及普遍化，但八〇年代的「个人电脑」，还只是用在工作场合。一直到九〇年代，「个人电脑」才跑到家庭里去，而且也还是叫做「个人电脑」，并无「家庭电脑」之名。当然，到了二〇〇七年，苹果公司更革命性地推出iPhone，iPhone事实上是一个可以放在衬衫口袋里的电脑；现在（二〇一九年），我们真正在一个「到处都有计算」、「到处都有电脑」的时代，但是「现在」是七〇年代的四十年后！

为什么夏伯特与现实脱节？关键是集成电路的功能。集成电路的功能与成本在二十世纪一直遵守「摩尔定律」——每两年一个「摩尔世代」。一直到九〇年代中期，集成电路才足够低价，而低价集成电路的功能才足够驱动电脑的家庭应用。在一九七八年要求有用而又合理价格的家庭电脑，至少早了六、七个「摩尔世代」，也就是十二至十四年。

上章我描述夏伯特对64K记忆体的「茫然」——听了我们几小时后，他还问：「你们真的在做吗？」就是这对技术现实的茫然，加上他刚愎自用的性格，使他高估我们能开发出「家庭电脑」的应用。他要「家庭电脑」算家帐，我们的「家庭电脑」只会算简单的家帐，而不会算他很在意的「现金流量」；他要「家庭电脑」报个人所得税，我们的「家庭电脑」只能报最简单的个人所得税，而买得起我们「家庭电脑」（零售价一千一百五十美元）的个人，其所得税没那么简单；他要「家庭电脑」教育小孩，我们「家庭电脑」的教育游戏不见得比售价五十美元的S&S高明。

<p class="en-para">In reality, the home computer was only part of Shepherd's "Ubiquitous Computation" vision. In Shepherd's vision, home computers would balance household budgets, file personal income taxes, educate children, store recipes, and so on. "Business computers" would sit in offices doing professional work.</p>
<p class="en-para">Shepherd's vision was farsighted — but at least a decade ahead of reality! In the real world, IBM launched the "personal computer" in 1981, legitimizing and popularizing the PC. But 1980s "personal computers" were still used only in work settings. It wasn't until the 1990s that "personal computers" made their way into homes — and they were still called "personal computers," not "home computers." Of course, by 2007, Apple more revolutionarily launched the iPhone — a computer that could fit in a shirt pocket. Now (2019), we are truly in an era of "computation everywhere" and "computers everywhere" — but "now" is forty years after the 1970s!</p>
<p class="en-para">Why was Shepherd disconnected from reality? The key was IC functionality. IC functionality and cost throughout the 20th century followed "Moore's Law" — one "Moore generation" every two years. It wasn't until the mid-1990s that ICs became cheap enough, and cheap ICs powerful enough, to drive home computer applications. Demanding a useful and reasonably priced home computer in 1978 was at least six or seven "Moore generations" too early — twelve to fourteen years.</p>
<p class="en-para">In the previous chapter, I described Shepherd's "bewilderment" about the 64K memory — after listening for hours, he still asked: "Are you really working on it?" It was this bewilderment about technological reality, combined with his stubborn personality, that caused him to overestimate our ability to develop "home computer" applications. He wanted the home computer to balance household budgets — ours could only do simple budgeting, not the "cash flow" analysis he cared about. He wanted it to file personal income taxes — ours could only handle the simplest returns, and the individuals who could afford our home computer ($1,150 retail) had far more complex taxes. He wanted it to educate children — its educational games were arguably no better than the $50 S&S.</p>
