> **Recruiting Shang-yi Chiang: The Ambition for Technology Leadership**
>
> When Brook resigned in April 1997 and I took over as General Manager, I was eager not only to deepen partnerships with key clients but also to make a mark in research and development. I believed that the quality and dedication of Taiwan's R&D talent was a genuine competitive advantage.
>
> Shortly after assuming the GM role, Zeng Fan-cheng recommended a brilliant R&D leader: Shang-yi Chiang. Chiang's credentials were exemplary—National Taiwan University bachelor's, Princeton master's, Stanford Ph.D.—with prior R&D experience at Texas Instruments and Hewlett-Packard. After a three-hour conversation with Chiang in Taiwan, I was thoroughly impressed and immediately offered him the position of VP of R&D. At that moment, I was already harboring ambitions to make TSMC the world's technology leader in semiconductors, but I decided against revealing this on our first meeting—it might frighten him away.
>
> Chiang joined on July 7, 1997. Now, I thought, was the time. The new VP of R&D should understand our long-term R&D objective.
>
> A few years later, Business Weekly vividly recounted this scene:
>
> > During Chiang's first meeting with Chairman Morris Chang, Chang came straight to the point, demanding that Chiang elevate TSMC's R&D capabilities to world-leading levels.
> >
> > New to the company and conscious that TSMC lagged the world's leading fabs by two years, looking at an R&D department of only 120 people and an annual budget under US$100 million, Chiang thought, "Being first in the second tier would be good enough." He carefully responded, "The R&D investment of a technology leader must be three times that of a follower."
> >
> > "The Chairman immediately caught my implication," Chiang recalled with a laugh. "He lectured me, saying that [the prior VP] wouldn't have answered that way—he knew TSMC aimed to be the technology leader. He probably thought I was hopeless."
>
> The goal of "technology leadership worldwide" was only realized in 2018 with the mass production of 7 nm—but setting that goal produced a byproduct: it solidified our commitment to "technology self-sufficiency." And the timing was right, because the next temptation was approaching.
