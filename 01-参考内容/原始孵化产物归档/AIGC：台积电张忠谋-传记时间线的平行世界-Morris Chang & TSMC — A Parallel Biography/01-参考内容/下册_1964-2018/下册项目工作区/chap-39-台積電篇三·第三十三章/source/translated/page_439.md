<p class="en-para">TSMC's moment had arrived, but we still could not find a way into Apple.</p>
<p class="en-para">November 9, 2010 was a Tuesday. At 6 p.m., I had just concluded a two-day fourth-quarter board meeting at TSMC's Taipei office. By half past six I was back at our home, where Sophie intercepted me at the door. Terry Gou had called that afternoon, she said—he was bringing an Apple vice president to dinner at eight.</p>
<p class="en-para">Terry Gou is Sophie's cousin; our families kept close touch. He is also the founder and chairman of Foxconn, Apple's principal assembler and a company with deep ties to Apple. When I heard Sophie's words, I knew immediately why the Apple vice president was coming. Apple had been unable to find a foundry partner; now Apple was knocking on our door.</p>
<p class="en-para">The guest Terry brought was Jeff Williams. At Apple he was not merely a vice president but the chief operating officer.</p>
<p class="en-para">Sophie prepared a few additional dishes for our two guests. Once we were seated, Jeff came straight to the point: he wanted TSMC to manufacture logic chips designed by Apple. He had clearly done considerable research on TSMC and understood that we were not the kind of unit-margin assembly house he was accustomed to working with. 'You will enjoy a forty percent gross margin,' he said.</p>
<p class="en-para">In fact, TSMC's gross margin had already reached forty to forty-five percent before he arrived, and I was pushing to raise it to fifty percent. His remark was meant to reassure us that he would not be overly harsh on price. I took it the other way: he would not be generous with price.</p>
<p class="en-para">I said nothing on the spot. This was our first meeting, and there were many things to discuss. The pricing question could wait.</p>
