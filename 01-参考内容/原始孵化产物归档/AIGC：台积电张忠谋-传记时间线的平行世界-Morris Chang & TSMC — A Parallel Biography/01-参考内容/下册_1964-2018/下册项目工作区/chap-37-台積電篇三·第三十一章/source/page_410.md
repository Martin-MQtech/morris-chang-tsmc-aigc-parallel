## 拒做「管理层收购」（二○○七年）

台积电在台湾成立第二年（一九八八年），在美国发生了金融史上首见的「管理层收购」（Management Buyout, MBO）案。一个大规模上市公司雷诺纳贝斯克（RJR Nabisco）的CEO，为了炒高公司的股价并增加自己的持股比例，与一「金融机构」结盟，企图以高于当时市价的价钱收购所有市面上该公司的股票——但仍由自己经营——这就是所谓「管理层收购」。这桩案子的结局呢？股价被炒高了，但收购者不是该CEO结盟的金融机构，而是另一家「私募基金」。CEO反而失去了他的职位。

虽然这件「管理层收购」案并没有成功，但此后不少此类案子都成功了。我没想到的是：居然「管理层收购」的可能性，也会发生在台积电头上。

二○○六年是台积电破纪录的一年——营收近百亿美元，营业净利近四十亿美元，当时市值（每股股价乘以股票总数）已超过五百亿美元。

二○○七年二月二日，高盛集团合伙人宋学仁来访，建议高盛银行结合别的金融机构，再加上向其他银行的贷款，便可与台积电管理阶层结盟做「管理层收购」。宋的说辞是：

（一）总共出资七百亿美元，收购市面上所有台积电股票，但管理層已拥有的股票除外。而这七百亿美元的资金约一半来自贷款。

（二）对管理阶层的好处：藉贷款的槓杆作用，可以增加我们的持股比例。

（三）贷款利息（当时约为八％）可以用不发股利省下来的现金支付。

（四）公司的资本支出、员工红利等都可以不变。

听了宋的说辞后，我答应考虑。一星期后我照原定的行程去旧金山做演讲，顺便去了纽约一趟。专管此类购并投资案的高盛资深合伙人康奈尔（Henry Cornell）及他的副手弗里曼（Rich Friedman）也是一位资深合伙人，来我纽约家讨论此案。他们的言辞与宋大致符合，但对此案是否能做成，似乎较宋保守。

<p class="en-para">In 1988, the second year after TSMC's founding in Taiwan, the United States experienced the first management buyout (MBO) case in financial history. The CEO of RJR Nabisco, a large publicly traded company, allied with a 'financial institution' in order to inflate the company's stock price and increase his own shareholding. He attempted to acquire all publicly traded shares at a premium to the market price—while retaining management control. That was the so-called management buyout. How did it end? The stock price was inflated, but the acquirer was not the financial institution allied with the CEO; it was another 'private equity fund.' The CEO instead lost his position.</p>
<p class="en-para">Although this management buyout did not succeed, many such cases succeeded afterward. What I never expected was that the possibility of a management buyout could arise for TSMC.</p>
<p class="en-para">2006 was a record year for TSMC—revenue approached ten billion dollars, operating profit approached four billion dollars, and the market capitalization (share price multiplied by total shares) had already exceeded fifty billion dollars.</p>
<p class="en-para">On February 2, 2007, Henry Song, a partner at Goldman Sachs, visited and suggested that Goldman combine with other financial institutions and secure loans from additional banks to ally with TSMC's management in a management buyout. Song's pitch was as follows:</p>
<p class="en-para">(1) A total of seventy billion dollars would be deployed to acquire all TSMC shares on the market, excluding those already held by management. Approximately half of this sum would come from borrowing.</p>
<p class="en-para">(2) Benefit to management: through the leverage of borrowing, our shareholding percentage could be increased.</p>
<p class="en-para">(3) Loan interest (then approximately eight percent) could be paid from cash saved by not paying dividends.</p>
<p class="en-para">(4) The company's capital expenditures, employee bonuses, and other items would remain unchanged.</p>
<p class="en-para">After hearing Song's pitch, I agreed to consider it. A week later, I traveled to San Francisco for a scheduled speech and also made a stop in New York. Henry Cornell, a senior partner at Goldman Sachs who specialized in mergers and acquisitions, and his deputy Rich Friedman, also a senior partner, came to my New York home to discuss the proposal. Their言辞 were broadly consistent with Song's, but they seemed more conservative than Song regarding whether the deal could actually be completed.</p>
